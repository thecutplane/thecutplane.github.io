EP02: How a submarine works (USS Thresher, 1963)

AI-assisted research, reviewed and approved by the channel editor on 2026-11-27.

138 facts checked. 138 verified.
Main source: Thresher II (SSN-593), Dictionary of American Naval Fighting Ships (NHHC)

The exact cause of the loss was never proven. The 1963 Court of Inquiry gave only the most probable sequence of events, and the Navy says the cause could not be determined conclusively.

Research notes

Main sources: the Naval History and Heritage Command (DANFS), the 2003 congressional statement of RADM Paul E. Sullivan on SUBSAFE, Arlington National Cemetery, NAVSEA and the U.S. Naval Institute Proceedings summaries of the 1963 Court of Inquiry. The Court of Inquiry said the loss was 'in all probability' caused by engine-room flooding, an electrically induced reactor shutdown and an air system that iced up; the Navy itself says the cause could not be determined conclusively. Acoustic analyst Bruce Rule argues for an electrical failure first; his estimates of how long the hull collapse lasted range from about a tenth of a second to about 47 milliseconds. The distance is given as about 220 miles east of Cape Cod (some sources say about 200 miles off the coast). The 15-ship figure is the initial search (Arlington National Cemetery); over the whole search, 28 warships and five oceanographic vessels took part (Andrews, 1964). The design collapse depth is 1,950 ft (one source rounds it to 1,900 ft). Pressures are our own calculations with NOAA's rule (+1 atmosphere every 33 ft, 14.7 psi): 579 psi at 1,300 ft, 869 psi at 1,950 ft, 3,742 psi at 8,400 ft (water pressure only). Building heights are architectural heights (CTBUH): Empire State Building 1,250 ft, Burj Khalifa 2,717 ft. The count of 16 non-combat losses between 1915 and 1963 is the Navy's (Sullivan); other counts exist depending on the criteria. Trieste found Thresher's debris in summer 1963 (photos released in September 1963); the well-known photo of the sail with the hull number 593 was taken in October 1964.
